Mapping Malaysia's Air Quality & Emission Realities

Linking greenhouse emissions to local PM₂.₅ pollution and public health impacts.

1. The Global and Regional Scale

Global and regional comparisons clarify Malaysia's position regarding shared Southeast Asian pollution challenges.

Developing countries across Asia and Africa face the highest global pollution risks.

Malaysia performs better than some regional neighbors but exceeds safe WHO health guidelines fourfold.

2. Tracking the Severe Haze Crisis

Peat fires, agricultural clearing, and transboundary winds regularly trigger severe, seasonal air degradation.

Major historical transboundary haze events trigger severe, irregular air pollution spikes across Malaysia.

PM₂.₅ consistently drives severe atmospheric anomalies, peaking dramatically during regional environmental spikes.

September 2019 marks Malaysia's most severe fine particulate spike in recent history.

3. Geographic Distribution and Urban Divergence

High industrial clustering along the West Coast drives Malaysia's sharp urban-rural pollution divide.

Industrialized West Coast states drive Malaysia’s highest fine particulate concentrations.

Selangor leads the nation in fine particulate pollution, while Sabah remains the cleanest.

Selangor’s integrated pollution profile comprehensively dwarfs both the national average and Labuan’s baseline.

Urban development directly correlates with significantly elevated fine particulate pollution across Malaysia.

Urban areas display a strong, direct correlation between vehicle emissions (NO₂) and dangerous particulates.

4. Dissecting the Greenhouse Gas Emissions Profile

Tracking structural greenhouse gas drivers highlights how economic infrastructure shapes long-term atmospheric composition.

The energy sector overwhelmingly drives national emissions, while LULUCF offsets carbon volumes.

Natural carbon sinks minimize net totals, but peat fires cause severe emission surges.

A sequential waterfall breakdown reveals how LULUCF successfully cancels out nearly two-thirds of Malaysia's greenhouse emissions.

Conclusion

Malaysia must transition from high-emission urban energy grids while aggressively protecting natural forest carbon sinks to ensure breathable air quality.
